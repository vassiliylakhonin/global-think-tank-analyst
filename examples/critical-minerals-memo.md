## Executive takeaway

**Key judgment (Moderate confidence):** Price volatility, while visible, is not the dominant risk. The dominant risk is **processing concentration** (mid-stream refining capacity sits in a small number of jurisdictions for several of these inputs). Diversifying upstream mining without addressing midstream processing produces a brittle resilience claim.

## Facts vs assessments

- **Fact:** Critical-minerals supply chains are typically multi-stage (mine → concentrate → refine → precursor → cell input). Risk attaches to each stage independently.
- **Fact:** Several jurisdictions classify a subset of these minerals as strategic and have published export-control or licensing instruments.
- **Assessment:** Midstream processing concentration is a stronger structural risk than mining concentration for most battery inputs.
- **Assessment:** "Diversified mining + concentrated refining" is the modal pattern in 2024–2026 procurement claims and tends to overstate resilience.
- **Assumption:** No regime-changing event in the 24-month horizon.

## Actor incentives and leverage

- **Producing-jurisdiction governments:** want to capture more value-add domestically → policy direction is toward local processing requirements.
- **Refining-jurisdiction governments:** want to retain industrial advantage and have export-restriction tools available as leverage.
- **Customers:** want price stability and supply continuity → reward suppliers with visible upstream diversification, even when midstream remains concentrated.
- **The firm:** wants resilience without paying for redundancy it does not need → temptation to optimize the procurement narrative rather than the structural exposure.

## Scenarios

1. **Steady-state concentration (most likely).** Midstream refining concentration persists. Volatility is price-led, not supply-led. Cost: occasional inventory pain.
2. **Targeted export instrument.** A producing or refining jurisdiction tightens an export instrument for a specific input. Cost: 1–2 quarter disruption for that input.
3. **Strategic decoupling acceleration.** Allied jurisdictions invest in domestic processing; supplier landscape bifurcates. Cost: dual-architecture procurement, slower contracts.
4. **Demand-side shock.** A buyer or technology shift reshapes input weighting (e.g., chemistry change reduces cobalt). Cost: stranded inventory or contracts.

## Options and trade-offs

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Diversify upstream mining only | Cheap, easy to communicate | Does not protect against scenarios 2 or 3 |
| Diversify across both upstream and midstream | Genuine resilience | Higher cost; some midstream alternatives are immature |
| Long-term contracts with processing-side hedges | Predictability | Locks in volume against possible chemistry shifts |
| Scenario 4 hedge: chemistry-flexible system architecture | Robust to demand-side shock | Engineering cost; product complexity |

## Watch-next indicators

- New export-licensing or quota instruments from producing or refining jurisdictions.
- Public commitments by allied governments to subsidize domestic processing capacity.
- Price-volatility regime change (sustained widening of bid-ask spreads on input contracts).
- Customer due-diligence requests escalating from upstream sourcing claims to midstream processing claims.
- Chemistry-shift signals from major OEMs (e.g., LFP vs NMC ratios in announced platforms).

## Confidence and key unknowns

- Confidence: **Moderate** on direction (processing risk > mining risk); **Low** on exact timing of any single scenario.
- Key unknowns: pace of midstream capacity build-out in allied jurisdictions; chemistry composition of next-cycle OEM platforms; specific export-instrument announcements.

## What would change the judgment

- A new export instrument from a refining-side jurisdiction covering inputs the firm uses at scale.
- A material chemistry shift announcement from a top-three customer.
- A coordinated allied subsidy package that materially reshapes midstream capacity within the 24-month horizon.
